[THESIS]

Black Studies as a Political Act

Stewart Fenderson argues in his article that Black Studies emerged during a period of expanding state repression and restructuring racial capitalism. The formation of Black Studies programs was not simply about representation in the academy, it was a direct response to intensifying systems of surveillance, policing, incarceration, and economic exclusion reshaping Black life in the late twentieth century.

Derecka Purnell's Becoming Abolitionists deepens this analysis by tracing her own political transformation and arriving at a clear conclusion: reform is insufficient. Dismantling these systems requires abolition, not adjustment of the existing structure.

"Mass incarceration and the racial wealth gap are not separate crises. They are interconnected outcomes of state policy, economic restructuring, and racial influence in law practices -- structural inequalities, not accidental ones."

Data from The Sentencing Project and the Brookings Institution confirms that racial disparities in incarceration and wealth remain an ongoing issue through 2024. Understanding their historical development reveals that these inequalities are structural rather than accidental.

[TOPICS]

Key Themes

State Repression

Federal urban policy in the 1960s funneled billions into policing Black neighborhoods under the cover of anti-poverty programs. Hinton shows that the War on Poverty became, in practice, a war on Black communities: expanding surveillance and criminalization rather than opportunity.

$2.3B

Authorized by the Demonstration Cities Act of 1966, framed as urban aid, but redirected toward policing and "control" of racially marginalized communities.

- Hinton, A War within Our Own Boundaries

Mass Incarceration

Angela Davis argues that prisons function not as crime deterrents but as warehouses for people discarded by the economy. The numbers bear this out: the U.S. prison population grew nearly 700% between 1972 and its 2009 peak, driven by policy choices, not crime rates.

5x

Black Americans are incarcerated at five times the rate of white Americans. Black Americans make up 38% of the prison population despite being 13% of the U.S. population.

- The Sentencing Project, 2024

Racial Capitalism

The Black-white wealth gap is not a product of individual choices, it is the direct result of redlining, exclusion from the GI Bill, discriminatory appraisals, and labor market barriers sustained over generations. Fenderson ties these economic structures directly to the rise of Black Studies as a field.

$240,120

The median wealth difference between white and Black households as of 2022, up $49,950 in just three years. Only 44% of Black Americans own homes vs. 73% of white Americans.

- Brookings Institution, 2024

The Case for Abolition

Purnell's Becoming Abolitionists traces her own shift from reform to abolition. Each attempted reform: body cameras, implicit bias training, consent decrees left the structure intact. The data on life sentences shows why: Black Americans make up 55% of those serving life without parole.

55%

Of people serving life without the possibility of parole are Black, the most extreme end of a system Purnell argues cannot be reformed from within.

- The Sentencing Project; Purnell, Becoming Abolitionists

Black Education & Resistance

Before, during, and after Reconstruction, Black communities built their own schools at enormous personal cost: paying tuition, donating labor, and pooling meager resources. Because access to education was inseparable from the meaning of freedom itself.

5,000+ Schools Built

Rosenwald built schools across the South between 1912 and 1932, funded largely by Black community contributions, a direct response to the failure of public institutions to educate Black children.

- JSTOR Daily, How Black Communities Built Their Own Schools

Historical Continuity

From the rollback of Reconstruction to the War on Drugs to modern mandatory minimums, each era has produced new mechanisms for the same outcome. One in five Black men born in 2001 is projected to be imprisoned in his lifetime. Down from one in three, but still a structural crisis.

1 in 5 Imprisoned

A fifth of Black men born in 2001 are projected to be imprisoned in their lifetime. For those born in 1981, it was 1 in 3. Progress exists, but the discrimination remains underlying and systematic.

- The Sentencing Project, One in Five Series
